# "We already collected the data" — Evidence Conversion

The most common and most expensive misunderstanding in this workflow.

**Research complete ≠ engineering complete ≠ regulatory complete.** When a
principal investigator says a dataset is finished, they mean the first one. Ask
**"complete to which standard?"** before you subtract anything from the schedule.

Subtract the *collection* time and cost. Do **not** subtract the conversion
work. Budget 6–9 months for it, and note that it usually runs parallel to the
QMS track, so it may not be on the critical path — check, don't assume.

## The five-item audit

Run all five before rescheduling. Each has a defined remedy, so this is a
half-day exercise that prevents a year of rework.

| # | Audit item | Why it matters | If it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **IRB scope** | Did the original informed consent cover commercial development, and transfer of the data to a company? The licence holder is a company; the data were collected by a university | IRB amendment; worst case, re-consent |
| 2 | **Version identity** | Is the version that was administered the same as the version being submitted? Stimulus duration, difficulty ladder, item count, or UI changes invalidate direct reuse of the norms | Freeze the submission version and run an equivalence study |
| 3 | **Test–retest data** | Cross-sectional data give percentiles but **cannot yield a Reliable Change Index**. Without RCI, any "declining trend" claim is unsupportable | Supplementary retest study (see below) |
| 4 | **Data traceability** | Raw data, administration records, and a pre-specified analysis plan must be auditable. Research-grade record-keeping often is not | Rebuild data-management documentation and audit trail |
| 5 | **Testing setting** | Community or school settings introduce noise, device variation, and differing supervision. This must be characterised and its effect on the norms argued | Sensitivity analysis; stratified reporting if needed |

## Item 3 is the one that usually bites

Ask the question in this exact form:

> **"Do you have the same individuals tested more than once?"**

Not "do you have reliability data" — people answer that with internal
consistency, which is a different thing entirely.

If the answer is no:

- A supplementary retest study becomes **mandatory**, not optional.
- Shape: n ≈ 60, two administrations 2–4 weeks apart, plus IRB lead time of
  2–3 months. Total ≈ 6–7 months from decision to analysed result.
- It needs a part-time clinical research coordinator — a role people assume they
  removed when the main collection finished.
- Run an n ≈ 20 pilot first to check the ICC magnitude before committing the
  full sample. Cognitive tasks often have mediocre test–retest reliability, and
  finding that out at n = 20 is much cheaper than at n = 60.

**Prepare the fallback in parallel:** a "percentile only, no trend claim"
version of the labelling. If the ICC comes back too low, the product still
reaches market — with a narrower claim. Deciding this in advance turns a
programme-threatening risk into a scoped one.

## Reporting the schedule impact honestly

When a conditional workstream becomes certain, the launch date often does not
move — because the work sits on a parallel track. It is tempting to report
"no schedule impact" and move on.

Report the **slack** instead:

> "Launch date unchanged. The clinical evaluation report moves from M7 to M10,
> so the buffer before submission drops from 5 months to 2. There is no room for
> a second delay on this track."

That is the information a decision-maker actually needs.
